## What Happens When You Have a Criminal Record? How an Immigration Lawyer Manages It.

Having a criminal record can feel like a heavy burden, especially when you are trying to navigate the immigration system. It might make you worry about your future in the United States. You might wonder if your dreams of a green card or citizenship are still possible.

The good news is that having a criminal record doesn't always mean your immigration journey is over. However, it does make things much more complicated. This is where an experienced immigration lawyer becomes incredibly important.

An immigration lawyer knows how to help people just like you. They can explain what your record means for your immigration case. They can also work hard to find solutions and help you achieve your goals.

### What is a Criminal Record?

A criminal record is an official document that lists your past arrests and convictions. It shows if you were found guilty of a crime or if you pleaded guilty. This record is kept by police departments and courts.

Even very old arrests can still appear on your criminal record. It's important to understand that even if charges were dropped, or if you were found not guilty, the arrest itself might still show up. This record follows you and can be accessed by government agencies.

For immigration purposes, your criminal record is very important. Immigration officials will look at it closely to decide if you are allowed to enter or stay in the U.S. They check to see if you meet all the rules.

### How a Criminal Record Affects Your Immigration Dreams

When you have a criminal record, it can create big problems for your immigration case. The law has many rules about who can come to the U.S. and who can stay. These rules are called "grounds of inadmissibility."

Some crimes make you "inadmissible," meaning you cannot get a visa or a green card. Other crimes might make you "deportable," meaning the government can send you home even if you have a green card. Even minor crimes can sometimes cause issues.

For example, a "crime involving moral turpitude" (CIMT) can be a major problem. These are crimes that involve bad conduct, like theft or fraud. Drug offenses, even for small amounts, can also lead to serious immigration consequences.

#### Different Types of Crimes and Their Impact

Not all crimes affect immigration in the same way. The type of crime, how serious it was, and the sentence you received all matter. Even crimes that seem minor can sometimes lead to big immigration headaches.

Let's look at some examples:

*   **Crimes Involving Moral Turpitude (CIMT):** These are serious. They often involve dishonesty or bad behavior, like shoplifting, assault, or fraud. Even one CIMT can make you inadmissible.
*   **Controlled Substance Offenses:** Any crime related to drugs, even possession of marijuana, can lead to inadmissibility. This is true even if the crime was a long time ago.
*   **Aggravated Felonies:** This is a group of very serious crimes under immigration law. If you have an aggravated felony, it often means you will be deported. It can also stop you from getting many immigration benefits.
*   **Multiple Criminal Convictions:** Having two or more convictions, even for less serious crimes, can also make you inadmissible. This is true if the total prison sentences add up to five years or more.

It's clear that the rules are complex and can be very confusing. You might not even know if your past actions count as one of these problem crimes. That's why getting help is so important.

For example, you might fill out a form incorrectly, or fail to mention a past arrest you thought was unimportant. These mistakes can be very costly. They can lead to accusations of fraud or misrepresentation, which are even bigger problems.

An immigration lawyer helps you avoid these pitfalls. They make sure your applications are correct and complete. They also prepare you for interviews and hearings, so you know what to expect.

### How an Immigration Lawyer Helps You with a Criminal Record

An immigration lawyer is your guide and defender when a criminal record stands in your way. They do many important things to help manage your situation. Their main goal is to protect your immigration status and help you achieve your goals.

#### Understanding Your Criminal Record

The very first thing an immigration lawyer will do is look closely at your criminal record. This means getting copies of all your court documents and police reports. They need to understand exactly what happened.

They will check the specific charges, the outcome of your case, and your sentence. This detailed review helps them figure out what immigration laws apply to you. It's like a detective figuring out all the pieces of a puzzle.

For example, a lawyer might see that a conviction for "assault" in your state is not considered a "crime involving moral turpitude" under immigration law. Or, they might find a mistake in your record that can be corrected. This careful review is the foundation of your entire case.

#### Evaluating Inadmissibility or Deportability Grounds

Once your lawyer understands your criminal record, they will tell you if you are "inadmissible" or "deportable." They will explain which specific immigration law applies to your crime. This might be because of a CIMT, a drug offense, or something else.

They will help you understand the legal terms and what they mean for you. For instance, they might tell you that a certain drug conviction makes you inadmissible under section 212(a)(2)(A)(i)(II) of the Immigration and Nationality Act. This precise knowledge is key. You can find more details on these laws on the USCIS website or similar official government sources.

Knowing the exact problem helps the immigration lawyer plan the best way forward. It helps them prepare your criminal immigration defense.

#### Exploring Criminal Immigration Defense Strategies

Sometimes, an immigration lawyer can work to change or fix your criminal record itself. This is a very important part of criminal immigration defense. They might work with your criminal defense attorney or even file motions in court.

*   **Post-Conviction Relief:** This means trying to undo or reduce the impact of your conviction. Your lawyer might look into:
    *   **Vacating a Plea:** If your old criminal lawyer did not tell you about the immigration risks of your plea, you might be able to ask the court to undo it. This is a complex legal process.
    *   **Expungement:** In some states, certain crimes can be "expunged." This means the record is sealed or erased. However, even an expunged record can still cause problems for immigration. An immigration lawyer knows when an expungement helps and when it doesn't.
    *   **Re-sentencing:** Sometimes, getting a shorter sentence can help. For example, if your sentence was reduced to less than one year, it might avoid certain immigration penalties.

Your immigration lawyer will know what options are available in your state. They will explain if changing your criminal record would genuinely help your immigration case. This is crucial because not all state-level remedies are recognized by immigration law.

#### Applying for Inadmissibility Waivers

If you are found to be inadmissible because of your criminal record, an immigration lawyer can help you apply for an "inadmissibility waiver." A waiver is like asking the government for forgiveness. It asks them to overlook your past crime so you can still get your immigration benefit.

To get a waiver, you usually need to show a few things:

1.  **Extreme Hardship:** You might need to prove that your U.S. citizen or green card holder family members would suffer "extreme hardship" if you were denied. This means showing very serious problems for them, not just normal difficulties.
2.  **Good Character:** You need to show that you are a good person now. This involves showing you have changed, are responsible, and contribute positively to your community.
3.  **Positive Factors:** Your lawyer will highlight all the good things about you, like your job, family ties, community involvement, and rehabilitation efforts.

There are different types of waivers, such as the I-601 waiver (for outside the U.S.) or the I-212 waiver (for those previously deported). Your immigration lawyer will know which waiver applies to your specific situation and how to build the strongest possible case for you. They help you gather all the necessary documents and write a compelling argument.

#### Representing You at Interviews and Hearings

Your immigration lawyer will not just fill out forms; they will be by your side. If you have an interview with USCIS (U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services) or a hearing in immigration court, your lawyer will be there. They will prepare you for questions you might be asked.

During the interview, they can clarify things and protect your rights. In court, they will argue your case to the judge. This representation is vital, as immigration proceedings can be very intimidating and confusing without legal help. They ensure your voice is heard and your case is presented fairly.

#### Providing Preventive Measures and Future Advice

An immigration lawyer also helps you plan for the future. They can advise you on how to avoid future immigration problems. This might include advice on travel, avoiding certain situations, and what to do if you are ever arrested again.

Their goal is not just to fix your current problem. They also want to make sure you stay on the right path for your immigration journey. This long-term guidance is invaluable.

### Practical Examples of an Immigration Lawyer's Work

Let's look at a few common situations where an immigration lawyer makes a real difference. These examples show how a criminal record, even a minor one, can create serious challenges. They also show how an immigration lawyer helps navigate these issues.

#### Example 1: The Green Card Applicant with an Old Minor Offense

Imagine Maria, who moved to the U.S. many years ago. She is now married to a U.S. citizen and applying for her green card. However, 15 years ago, she was arrested for shoplifting and received a small fine. She thought it was no big deal, as it was so long ago.

When she goes for her green card interview, the officer brings up the shoplifting charge. They say it's a "crime involving moral turpitude" and that she is inadmissible. Maria is terrified and doesn't know what to do. The officer tells her she needs an I-601 waiver.

Her immigration lawyer steps in. The lawyer carefully reviews the old court documents. They confirm it is indeed a CIMT. The lawyer then helps Maria apply for the I-601 inadmissibility waiver. They gather letters from Maria's U.S. citizen husband, explaining the extreme hardship he would face if Maria were denied and had to leave. They also collect evidence of Maria's good character since the incident. The lawyer successfully argues that Maria has been a model citizen for years and deserves the waiver.

#### Example 2: The Visa Holder Arrested While on a Visa

Meet Chen, who came to the U.S. on a student visa. While here, he was arrested for a minor drug possession charge. He panicked and pleaded guilty quickly, thinking it would make the problem disappear. However, his plea caused a major issue for his visa status.

He realizes his visa might be canceled, and he could be deported. He quickly contacts an immigration lawyer. The lawyer explains that any drug offense, even a minor one, makes him inadmissible and likely deportable. The lawyer immediately advises Chen not to leave the U.S. without discussing it first.

The immigration lawyer then works with a criminal immigration defense specialist. They review Chen's criminal case. They discover that in his state, there might be a chance to "vacate" his plea because his first lawyer didn't warn him about the immigration consequences. The lawyer helps Chen file a motion to undo his guilty plea. If successful, this could remove the immigration problem entirely, allowing him to stay and continue his studies.

#### Example 3: The Citizenship Seeker with an Expunged Crime

Consider David, a lawful permanent resident for over 10 years. He is finally ready to apply for U.S. citizenship. Years ago, he had a felony conviction for a non-violent crime, but his record was "expunged" by the state court. He believes it's no longer an issue.

He goes to an immigration lawyer to prepare his N-400 citizenship application. The immigration lawyer explains that even if a crime is expunged at the state level, it often still counts for immigration purposes. They tell David he must disclose the expunged felony on his application.

The lawyer advises David that his felony conviction makes him ineligible for citizenship because it falls within the required "good moral character" period. They advise him to wait longer before applying, or if the crime was an "aggravated felony," they inform him he might be deportable and help him explore other options like an inadmissibility waiver if applicable or cancellation of removal. This careful review saves David from a potential denial and possible deportation proceedings.

#### Example 4: The Person Facing Deportation Due to a Crime

Sarah has had a green card for several years. She was recently convicted of a serious assault charge, which is considered an "aggravated felony" under immigration law. Immigration authorities have now started deportation (removal) proceedings against her.

Sarah's immigration lawyer immediately gets to work. The lawyer first reviews the criminal conviction to ensure it is indeed an aggravated felony under immigration law. They explore if there are any post-conviction relief options that could change the criminal conviction's immigration impact. If that's not possible, they look for any available defenses in immigration court.

The lawyer might argue that Sarah qualifies for "cancellation of removal for certain permanent residents." To do this, they would need to prove she has lived in the U.S. for a long time, has strong family ties, and has shown good moral character for years, and that her removal would cause extreme hardship to qualifying U.S. citizen family members. This is a very difficult process, but her lawyer provides her best chance to fight deportation.

### Common Questions About Criminal Records and Immigration

Many people have similar questions when dealing with a criminal record and immigration. Here are some of the most common ones.

#### Can a DUI (Driving Under the Influence) Affect My Immigration?

Yes, absolutely. While a single DUI might not always make you inadmissible for all benefits, it can still cause problems. Multiple DUIs can be seen as a sign of poor moral character, which can affect applications for a green card or citizenship. A DUI can also lead to issues if you try to travel outside the U.S. and re-enter.

#### What If My Crime Was Long Ago?

Even very old crimes can affect your immigration case. Immigration law doesn't always have a "statute of limitations" like criminal law. This means a crime from 20 or 30 years ago can still be held against you. You must always disclose all past arrests and convictions, no matter how old.

#### Do I Need to Tell Immigration About ALL My Arrests?

Yes, you must tell immigration about *all* your arrests, even if the charges were dropped, you were found not guilty, or the record was expunged. Not disclosing an arrest can be seen as a lie or misrepresentation. This is a very serious immigration offense. It can lead to a lifetime ban from the U.S.

#### What is a "Crime Involving Moral Turpitude" (CIMT)?

A CIMT is a crime that shows bad behavior or evil intent. Common examples include theft, fraud, robbery, murder, and some types of assault. There's no single list, and courts often decide on a case-by-case basis. Your immigration lawyer can determine if your crime falls into this category. The interpretation of CIMT can vary and is a complex area of immigration law.

#### What If I Was Young When I Committed the Crime?

If you were a minor (under 18) when you committed a crime, sometimes it is treated differently. For example, some crimes committed under the age of 18 might not lead to inadmissibility, especially if the crime was a long time ago. However, this is not always the case. It is important to discuss your age at the time of the crime with your immigration lawyer.

### The Importance of Honesty and Full Disclosure

It is absolutely critical to be completely honest with your immigration lawyer and with immigration officials. Do not try to hide any part of your criminal history. Even a seemingly minor detail you leave out could be seen as misrepresentation.

Hiding information or lying on immigration forms can lead to severe consequences. This includes denial of your application, a permanent ban from the U.S., or even criminal charges. Your immigration lawyer needs to know everything to help you properly.

Remember, your lawyer is on your side. They need the full picture to prepare the best possible criminal immigration defense for you. They can only help you if you are honest about everything.
